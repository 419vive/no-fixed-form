An earthquake starts long before the ground begins to shake. Deep below us, tectonic plates keep moving. Along a fault, friction can hold the rocks in place while stress builds. When that stress overcomes the friction, the fault slips. Stored energy travels outward as seismic waves. The first waves are usually the fastest: P waves. Slower S waves follow. At the surface, other waves can produce strong rolling motion. But magnitude is only part of the story. Distance, depth, local soil, and the way buildings are constructed all affect what people experience. Soft sediments can amplify shaking. Two places hit by the same earthquake can feel very different motion. We cannot predict the exact time and place of the next major earthquake. Understanding the process helps us prepare for it. The lesson beneath our feet is simple: a quiet surface does not mean a motionless planet.
